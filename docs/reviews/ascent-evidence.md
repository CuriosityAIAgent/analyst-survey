# What the evidence says about asking this well

The evidence base for rewriting The Ascent's questions. Each finding below comes from a source that was opened and then checked by a second, independent pass. Where the published version of a paper gives different numbers from an earlier working paper, the published numbers are used. One further finding failed that check and is left out. Evidence strength is marked in square brackets: meta-analysis, RCT (randomised experiment), field study, survey, review, theory, or practitioner. Where this document refers to "the deck", it means the working group's nine-slide discussion guide of 25 September 2026. Where it refers to "the current flow", it means `src/content/flow.json` as of commit `dbc4fc6`.

**In one paragraph:** Three things change. First, ask about specific episodes and how they were arranged, not ratings or counts. Graduates cannot accurately report how much a programme developed them. Self-assessed learning tracks how much people liked something more closely than what they learned. People rewrite their "before" selves to fit a story of change. Recalled practice volume is the weakest measure in the expertise literature. And people misjudge, sometimes in the wrong direction, what AI did to their own speed and learning. So ask what they did, in which moment, who was there and when. Second, a task is not the unit that develops a junior. What develops them is the arrangement around the task: whether they produced a first answer before seeing one, whether they interpreted the work as well as assembling it, whether someone narrated their reasoning and gave feedback on the junior's judgement, and whether the task got them into the room. That turns "which reps stay human" into two questions: who owned the framing and the final call, and what access the task bought. Third, the format is part of the measurement. Forced trade-offs beat importance ratings. Item-specific scales beat agree/disagree. Screener questions have to come before follow-ups, or "no" becomes the way to shorten the survey. Drag sliders and illustrations on answer screens add noise. Several of these points cut against the current build.

## How judgement is actually built

**1. Feedback helps or harms depending on what it is about, not how much of it there is.** [meta-analysis] Across 607 effect sizes, feedback improved performance on average (d = .41), but more than a third of feedback interventions made performance worse. Feedback worked less well the more it moved attention off the task and onto the self, for example standing, rating or comparison with peers. A later synthesis of 994 effects found that feedback containing rich information on the task, the process and self-regulation had d = 0.99, against 0.46 for plain correction and 0.24 for reward or punishment. Peer feedback (0.85) beat teacher feedback (0.47), though the paper warns that the peer estimate rests on limited data. Both bodies of evidence come mainly from education.
*Sources:* Kluger & DeNisi 1996; Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie 2020.
*So we ask:* what the feedback was about ("the work or my reasoning" / "me, my rating or how I compared"), whether it said where to go, how I was doing and what to do next, and who gave it. Peers and Associates a year or two ahead are listed alongside Advisors and managers. We include "feedback that set me back" as an answer. If the deck's pass/fail "readiness checks" go ahead, we also ask how being assessed felt compared with being coached.

**2. Stretch builds skill only up to a point, unless feedback is available.** [field study] Across 225 on-the-job experiences of 60 managers, developmental challenge had diminishing returns. At high levels of challenge, access to feedback offset those diminishing returns. The sample is small.
*Source:* DeRue & Wellman 2009.
*So we ask:* for each experience a respondent names, how much of a stretch it was (unfamiliar, high-stakes, across the franchise) and whether anyone debriefed it. Include "thrown in too deep with no one to debrief", so over-stretch can appear as a dampener.

**3. In cognitive work, the expert's reasoning is invisible unless someone deliberately shows it.** [practitioner framework, no effect sizes] Cognitive apprenticeship calls for "the teacher's thinking [to] be made visible to the students and the student's thinking [to] be made visible to the teacher". The methods are modelling, coaching, scaffolding that is withdrawn over time, articulation, reflection and exploration. Learners should see the whole task before its parts.
*Source:* Collins, Brown & Holum 1991.
*So we ask:* not only "did you attend client meetings", but whether the Advisor shared their plan beforehand, whether they explained afterwards why they said what they said, and whether the Analyst was asked to explain their own reasoning before it was corrected. The deck's observe / prepare / present / own ladder is scaffolding that is withdrawn over time, so we ask whether support was removed too early, too late or never.

**4. Intuition is built only where feedback is fast and unambiguous. Confidence is not evidence of skill.** [review] Expert intuition needs a regular environment and long practice with feedback that is "both rapid and unequivocal". The authors name finance professionals as a group whose feedback on long-term judgements is "delayed, sparse, and ambiguous", which breeds overconfidence.
*Source:* Kahneman & Klein 2009.
*So we ask:* The deck's "when did you first feel genuinely effective?" measures confidence. We pair it with "what could you then do that you couldn't before?" and ask whether the respondent ever saw how a recommendation they worked on turned out. This also gives a test for which reps should stay human: reps with quick, clear human feedback, such as a client's live reaction or a senior's immediate pushback, teach by doing. Long-horizon portfolio calls teach little by doing, whoever does them.

**5. Counting practice predicts little in professional work, and recalled practice is inflated.** [meta-analysis] Deliberate practice explained about 24% of performance variance in games, 23% in music and 16% in sports, but about 1% (not significant) in professions. It explained less as the environment became less predictable. Retrospective interviews produced much stronger practice-to-performance links than practice logs kept at the time (about 20% against 5%). Figures are from the published corrigendum. The direction of every result is the same as in the original paper.
*Source:* Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald 2014.
*So we ask:* not "how many reps did it take?", which is in the deck. We anchor on an episode ("the first meeting where you led a section: what had you done before it?") and on how the practice was arranged: who reviewed it and whether the reasoning was explained. The survey should separate kinds of rep, not count them.

**6. What felt hard at the time often taught more, and learners misread smooth progress as learning.** [review] Spacing, interleaving, varied practice, generating answers and being tested slow apparent progress but improve retention and transfer. Participants who did better under interleaved practice still believed blocked practice had worked better. The authors call this an "illusion of mastery". Most of the evidence comes from labs and classrooms.
*Source:* Bjork & Bjork 2023.
*So we ask:* keep the deck's "what made you uncomfortable at the time, but ultimately made you better?". Keep "felt useful then" apart from "still draw on now". Asking years later helps, because respondents are judging from what they retained rather than from how smooth it felt.

**7. People learn mainly from assignments, hardships and other people. The "70-20-10" split has no solid basis.** [field study; review] McCall, Lombardo and Morrison asked 191 successful executives to "identify at least three key events in your career, things that made a difference in the way you manage now. What happened? What did you learn from it (for better or worse)?" They found learning came mainly from challenging assignments, hardships and other people, and that "different experiences teach different lessons". Clardy's review of the 70% claim found "sloppy scholarship, inconsistent conceptualizations, and fundamental research protocol problems" and concluded the rule "needs to be set aside". The book itself was not opened; the prompt comes from a CCL retrospective.
*Sources:* McCall, Lombardo & Morrison 1988; Clardy 2018.
*So we ask:* borrow the structure of McCall's prompt (event, what happened, lesson) and keep "(for better or worse)". Use assignments / hardships / other people / formal training as a checklist for "where did this capability come from?". Don't build 70/20/10 into answer options or into how redesigned time is sized.

### In wealth management specifically

**8. The industry's own bar for Lead Advisor is an outcome with clients, not a skill score.** [practitioner, 30 firms] The CFP Board's career-path guide sets promotion to Lead Advisor partly on having "demonstrated the ability to earn the trust of clients" and having "clients who are willing to work with her or him independently". It also includes being seen as knowledgeable and expert. It says Analysts "very rarely actively participate in client meetings". It names "tenure-driven promotions", "inconsistent promotions" and "unrealistic assessments" as common failures.
*Source:* CFP Board / The Ensemble Practice 2019.
*So we ask:* for dated relational milestones: when a client first contacted you directly instead of your senior, and when a client first accepted a recommendation you presented. And: "Before promotion, did anyone tell you plainly what you were not yet ready for?" (Yes, specifically / Vaguely / No).

**9. The credentialing body counts supervised, full-process client work as worth a third more than its hours.** [practitioner; draft standard] The CFP Board's draft standard accepts 4,000 hours in place of 6,000. The condition is that all 4,000 involve personal work with individual clients, cover all seven stages of the planning process, and are directly supervised and attested by a CFP professional.
*Source:* CFP Board draft Competency Standards, 21 April 2026.
*So we ask:* for each capability area, what role the respondent held and whether a named senior reviewed it, not time spent. Ask specifically about the stages that are easy to skip, presenting and implementing.

**10. Discovery and listening are a separate skill, learned late and mostly by watching.** [practitioner] Kitces describes four skill areas that are usually acquired in order: technical, then relationship, then business development, then management. In a Kitces podcast Carl Richards calls discovery questioning "the golf swing of our business", a skill to practise and record. Kitces' own survey finds advisors in their first five years spend about 13% of the week in client meetings. Top-productivity teams spend about 24%, against 17% for typical ones. The survey data are from US independent advisors.
*Sources:* Kitces 2016, 2019; Kitces & Richards 2021; Tenenbaum 2025.
*So we ask:* add a discovery rep to the activity list ("asked the client an open question and followed the answer"). Ask separately which piece of work improved your judgement and which first got you invited into a client conversation. Use the time-share figures only to set answer brackets, not as benchmarks shown to respondents.

**11. What predicts performance in client-facing sales is mostly learnable, and unclear expectations hurt.** [meta-analysis; meta-analysis] Across sales studies from 1982 to 2008, the strongest predictors were selling-related knowledge (β .28) and adaptiveness (.27). Role ambiguity was a strong negative (−.25). Separately, emotional-intelligence training has a moderate positive effect across 58 studies. Published studies show larger effects than dissertations, which suggests some publication bias.
*Sources:* Verbeke, Dietz & Verwaal 2011; Mattingly & Kraiger 2019.
*So we ask:* "As an Analyst, how clear were you on what you'd be judged on for promotion?" Avoid "which skills can't be taught?", which invites answers about fixed traits that the evidence does not support. Ask where the respondent practised the hardest client moment they later handled well, and who debriefed them.

**12. Emotional elements make up a large share of what clients value in advice.** [survey] Vanguard found emotional elements, such as trust, personal connection and "trusting that the advisor will put my needs first", made up about 40% of advised investors' perceived value of advice. The release gives no sample size, and the investors are not private bank clients.
*Source:* Madamba & Pagliaro, Vanguard 2020.
*So we ask:* when respondents sort tasks into human-led or AI-led: "Did this task build the client's trust in you, or only produce something the client used?"

**13. Keeping juniors in support roles too long limits them. Peer programmes that fixed this moved people onto teams earlier and broadened their exposure.** [survey; practitioner] Cerulli's Stephen Caruso warns that "keeping rookies in a support role for too long can limit their growth". Cerulli's training managers name time as the main constraint: 73% cite "time required to learn the business" and 67% say "daily instruction consumes too much time". Merrill says it expects 75% of its roughly 2,400 trainees to graduate, against an industry average of about 30% that its own executives quote. It credits a redesign that placed trainees on advisor teams sooner and gave them earlier access to the full product range. This is company-reported and uncontrolled. The Cerulli figure of about 72% rookie failure measures industry-wide production thresholds and should not be used in J.P. Morgan framing.
*Sources:* Cerulli 2023 and 2024 releases; Funds Society 2026; Rozen, AdvisorHub 2026.
*So we ask:* "When did you first own an outcome with a client? Year 1 / 2 / 3 / 4+ / not before promotion", then "too early, about right, or too late?". Also: "How many months in before you were embedded with the team whose clients you later served?" and "Before promotion, which of investments / lending / banking / trust & estates / planning had you worked on for a live client?".

### The experience types the survey should ask about

The research points to arrangements, not tasks. These are the types to ask about:

1. **Stretch with a debrief.** Unfamiliar or high-stakes work with feedback available afterwards (2).
2. **First answer before seeing one.** Producing your own view from a blank page before a senior's answer, a template or a tool's output (6; Bastani, Randazzo in the next section).
3. **Interpreting, not only assembling.** Saying what the analysis meant for the client and getting feedback on that reasoning (Anthony, next section).
4. **Reasoning made visible.** A senior's plan explained before the meeting and their choices explained after it (3).
5. **Fast, clear feedback from a real person.** A client's live reaction, a senior's immediate pushback, and seeing how a recommendation turned out (4).
6. **Ownership that increases, with support withdrawn at the right time.** Observe, prepare, present, own, with a named reviewer (3, 9, 13).
7. **Hardship and discomfort.** Setbacks and difficult moments, reported "for better or worse" (6, 7).
8. **Working alongside a strong senior.** Proximity to the best Advisors, and the trust that led them to give the junior more (Beane, Ide, next section).
9. **Discovery with a client.** Asking and listening, not only presenting (10).

## What AI does to it

This section separates three kinds of evidence: what experiments show, what field studies in comparable settings show, and what is argued from correlation or theory. None of it studies private bank Analysts.

### Shown in randomised experiments

- **Unguided AI raised practice scores and lowered unaided ability, and learners did not notice.** [RCT] Nearly 1,000 high-school maths students took part. With a standard GPT-4 interface, practice grades rose 48% against controls. On the later exam taken without AI, the same students scored 17% lower than controls. A tutor version that gave teacher-designed hints instead of answers lifted practice grades 127%, and its exam result was no different from controls. Students using the standard version did not perceive that they had learned less. *Source:* Bastani et al. 2025. *Limit:* school students, maths.
- **With AI, junior engineers learned less of a new library, and checking code was the worst-affected skill.** [RCT, preprint] In a trial with 52 mostly junior engineers, the AI group scored 50% on an immediate mastery quiz against 67% for the control group (d = 0.74). They were not significantly faster. The largest gap was in debugging. People who asked conceptual questions, or generated code and then asked for explanations, kept most of their learning. People who delegated wholesale scored lowest. *Source:* Shen & Tamkin 2026. *Limit:* a company preprint, not peer-reviewed. The subgroups by usage pattern had 2 to 7 people each, so "how you use it matters" is suggestive, not established.
- **AI made output look better even when the answer was wrong.** [RCT] BCG consultants were randomised, with about 385 on tasks within AI's capability and 373 on a task beyond it. On 18 realistic tasks within AI's capability, the AI group completed 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, at more than 40% higher quality, and the bottom half by skill gained most. The other task needed a judgement AI gets wrong. There the control group was correct 84.5% of the time, against 60% and 70.6% in the two AI arms. The AI users' write-ups were still graded more coherent and persuasive (+1.05 to +1.47 on a 10-point scale) "regardless of the correctness of their answer". *Source:* Dell'Acqua et al. 2026, Organization Science.
- **People misjudge what AI did to their own speed.** [RCT] Sixteen experienced developers took 19% longer on real tasks when AI was allowed. Afterwards they still believed it had made them 20% faster. *Source:* METR 2025. *Limit:* small sample, early-2025 models.

### Shown in field studies

- **When the workflow allowed it, a quarter of juniors handed the thinking to AI and built no skill.** [field experiment] 244 junior BCG staff solved a business case with GPT-4. 60% worked with AI throughout ("Cyborgs"); they gained AI skill and kept their expertise in the task. 14% framed the problem themselves and gave AI bounded subtasks ("Centaurs"); they built expertise and made the most accurate recommendations. 27% delegated the analysis and evaluation ("Self-Automators"); they showed "no discernible skilling", and their work was "fast and polished but lacked depth". *Source:* Randazzo et al. 2025. *Limit:* one case. The authors note that handing off routine work is not always a problem.
- **In an investment bank, juniors who only assembled the analysis and left interpretation to seniors developed less.** [field study, ethnography] Anthony spent two years observing four groups in an investment bank. In some groups juniors assembled analyses and only seniors interpreted them ("partitioning"). In others juniors did both, and seniors gave feedback on the juniors' reasoning ("co-construction"). Partitioning groups treated the tools as black boxes, and this "constrained the development of junior members' expertise, with consequences for their career trajectories". *Source:* Anthony 2021. This is the closest setting to ours, and it predates generative AI.
- **When technology let the expert work alone, trainees lost their practice and learned through unofficial routes.** [field study, ethnography] In robotic surgery the lead surgeon could operate alone. Residents became optional and got "ten to twenty times less practice". The minority who became competent did so through "shadow learning": simulators, video, and operating with minimal supervision. Beane's later work names three conditions for building skill: challenge, complexity and connection (the trust that leads seniors to grant access). *Source:* Beane 2019. The full paper was paywalled; the figures come from the university's summary of it.
- **Seniors learning a new technology tended to take the junior's development with them.** [field study, ethnography] In surgery and investment banking, seniors learned new technology through "inverted apprenticeships", in which juniors helped them. Most of these routes "undermined the learning of those most junior". *Source:* Beane & Anthony 2024.
- **Trained experts lost unaided skill once a tool did the noticing.** [field study, observational] In Polish colonoscopy centres, endoscopists' adenoma detection rate in procedures done without AI fell from about 28% to 22% after routine AI use began. *Source:* Budzyń et al. 2025. *Limit:* found by accident in an observational sub-study; the authors call it hypothesis-generating.
- **Counterweight: where AI passes on how the best people work, it can speed learning.** [field study] Among 5,172 customer-support agents, an AI assistant raised productivity 15% on average and about 30% for less experienced and less skilled agents. Agents with two months' tenure and AI performed like agents with more than six months and no AI. During AI outages, previously exposed agents stayed faster than before, which suggests real learning; the authors say these estimates are noisy. The most skilled agents saw small quality declines, and the authors raise the risk of "over-relying on AI recommendations beyond what is optimal in the long run". *Source:* Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond 2025. *Limit:* scripted, high-feedback work, far more routine than advisory work.
- **AI practice partners help, but they don't teach you to read people.** [field study, archival] When chess computers spread, players improved because machines stood in for scarce training partners. They were "not a perfect substitute", because players who trained with them did not learn to exploit idiosyncratic human mistakes. *Source:* Gaessler & Piezunka 2023.

### Argued from correlation, theory or company claims

- **The missing rung.** [field study, correlational] In US payroll data, employment of 22 to 25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations fell 16% relative to others, while employment of experienced workers held steady. The authors suggest AI automates "the codifiable, checkable tasks that historically justified entry-level headcount". That explanation is a hypothesis. The data are correlational and not specific to banking. *Source:* Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen 2025.
- **What matters is who juniors sit next to, not how many juniors there are.** [theory] In an economic model, novices learn tacit knowledge by working alongside experts. Automating entry-level work can lower long-run growth "even without reducing entry-level employment" if it moves novices away from the best experts. This is a model with no new data. *Source:* Ide 2026.
- **The firm's own goal.** [company claim] J.P. Morgan says its GenAI "Coach" will let advisors "expand their client rosters by 50%". This comes from JPM's account of the HBS case, which was not opened. If true, it squeezes the time an Analyst has to develop. The survey should not assume AI frees time for development. *Source:* J.P. Morgan Private Bank 2025.

### What this means for "which reps stay human"

What the evidence supports: AI that hands juniors the answer can raise their output and lower what they can do alone. It makes wrong answers look better. People cannot tell from the inside that either is happening. It supports less firmly that how AI is used matters more than whether it is used, and that AI designed to give hints or model expert practice can speed learning. It does not show anything about private bank Analysts in particular. It does not show that any specific task must stay human.

So the survey should not ask "should AI do task X?". It should ask about the properties that made a rep developmental. A rep has a claim to stay human-led if it has one or more of these properties:

1. **It is where the junior forms a view before seeing one:** framing the question and making the final call (Bastani, Randazzo, Anthony).
2. **It teaches the junior to spot a wrong answer,** which they will need in order to review AI output. People can't review what they never learned to build (Dell'Acqua, Shen & Tamkin).
3. **It gets the junior into the room or alongside the Advisor,** even if the content itself was low-value (Beane; the Canaries hypothesis).
4. **It gets fast, clear feedback from a real person,** such as a client's reaction or a senior's pushback (Kahneman & Klein; Gaessler & Piezunka).
5. **It builds the client's trust in the junior** (Vanguard; the CFP Lead Advisor criteria).

Against these, allow for the case where a tool could have taught faster: examples of what good looks like, product knowledge, sample client language (Brynjolfsson et al.; the Bastani tutor arm). Capture the mode of use, not just the label. For example: "When a tool drafted this, what did you do next? Used it as-is / checked the numbers / rebuilt the logic / asked why." This matches the "what you still do" column of the AI ladder in `docs/reviews/ascent-ai-ladder.md`. Record the respondent's cohort year, because tool exposure differs across cohorts. Treat any answer to "would AI have made you less prepared?" as an attitude, not evidence.

## Asking so the answers are true

### Episodes over ratings

- **Self-assessed development measures liking more than learning.** [meta-analysis] Self-assessed knowledge correlates .34 with measured learning, but .51 with satisfaction and .59 with motivation. Nearly a third of evaluation studies wrongly treated it as evidence of learning. *Source:* Sitzmann et al. 2010. So "how much did X develop you?" mostly measures whether the respondent liked X, or liked the manager who gave it. Report such items as "graduates attribute", never as "X develops".
- **People rewrite their "before" to fit a story of change.** [review] After a study-skills course with no objective effect, participants recalled their earlier skills as worse than they had rated them at the time, "apparently confirming the intervention's success", even with incentives to be accurate. *Source:* Schwarz 2007, describing Conway & Ross. So avoid "how ready were you before vs after?". Ask for dated firsts ("When did you first lead a client meeting? Which year of the programme? Who was in the room?"), which are harder to rewrite.
- **A single overall rating reflects the peak and the end.** [meta-analysis] Retrospective summaries are driven by the most intense moment and the final one (r = .58). Duration has essentially no effect. For long, mixed experiences the average matters more after a delay. *Source:* Alaybek et al. 2022; Strijbosch et al. 2019. So a global "How well did A2A prepare you?" (the current `prep` card) mostly reflects one standout moment and the promotion year. It should not be the headline metric. Ask about phases, and ask about the long middle stretch separately.
- **Critical incidents work when the prompt asks what the person did in one bounded episode.** [review] In one study, changing "how this employee behaved" to "what this employee did" moved answers away from personality and produced a much broader range of incidents. Asking for "the most recent" incident prevents recall of only the vivid ones. Full detail signals accurate memory; vague detail signals poor memory. *Source:* Flanagan 1954. Flanagan's method is for observers, so applying it to self-report is an extrapolation. So: "Think of one moment after which you could do something with a client you couldn't do before. What did you do in it?", paired with "the last client meeting you prepared for as an Associate", and one short free-text detail field as a quality check.
- **Hindsight produces single-cause stories.** [review] Hindsight bias narrows attention to one causal story. Prompting people to consider alternative causes reduces it. *Source:* Roese & Vohs 2012. So after someone names their biggest accelerator, ask "What else might have mattered as much?", with a short multi-select. Also ask "What did you think was a waste of time then that you now value?" and the reverse.

### Name the thing in the scale

- **Agree/disagree items measure worse than item-specific ones.** [RCT, split-ballot] In representative surveys, agree/disagree questions produced much lower measurement quality than the same questions with item-specific options, and adding more agree/disagree points did not help. Acquiescence is the likely mechanism, but the paper does not establish it. *Source:* Saris et al. 2010. So instead of "A2A prepared me well: Strongly agree ... Strongly disagree", ask "When you first led a client meeting as an Advisor, how prepared were you? Not at all / Partly / Mostly / Fully / I haven't yet."

### Forced choice where there is an obvious right answer

- **Forced-choice formats sharply reduce self-flattering answers, if the options are balanced.** [meta-analysis] Forced-choice measures showed score inflation of d = 0.06 under high stakes, far less than single-statement measures. They resisted faking best when options within a block were matched on social desirability. *Source:* Cao & Drasgow 2019. "Which reps must stay human?" has an obvious right-sounding answer: everything client-facing is precious, all admin is waste. Use trade-offs between items of similar appeal, for example "build the portfolio analysis yourself" vs "review AI's version and defend it". Don't set "lead a client meeting" against "reconcile a spreadsheet".
- **Best-worst scaling is quicker than ratings and more stable than ranking.** [method comparison; RCT] Best-worst scaling reproduced a validated values structure in less respondent time than ratings. Across repeat measurements its preferences were more stable than rankings (Kendall τ 0.69 vs 0.47), and ranks in the middle were mostly noise: 81% of rank-6 answers changed. *Sources:* Lee, Soutar & Louviere 2008; Liang, Glover & Hawkins 2026. So use a short, balanced best-worst exercise ("most needed more of" / "most should be freed from") in place of drag-to-rank. The current `fuel` card asks respondents to drag five items into order. Each item must appear equally often and be paired with each other item about equally often. An "activity missing from this list" box keeps the deck's rule that missing activities are findings.

### Third person for the sensitive questions

- **Asking about "people like you" draws out more candour, but the answers are the respondent's own views.** [RCT] Indirect questioning reduced social-desirability bias on sensitive items and had no effect on neutral ones. Respondents attributed their own views to the target, and did so more readily for an out-group target. *Source:* Fisher 1993. Use it for questions about manager quality, luck, busywork and readiness checks: "For Analysts on teams like yours, what most often gets in the way of client exposure?". Report the answers as perceptions. Keep the reference group identical for every respondent, or the framing itself will create differences between teams.

### Catching staging and careless answers

"Staging" here means answering for the sponsor rather than from experience, or clicking through without reading.

- **Different kinds of careless answering need different checks.** [field study and simulation] Random and patterned careless answering need different checks: consistency pairs, instructed items, outlier checks and response time. About 10 to 12% of students on a long, mandatory survey were flagged. *Source:* Meade & Craig 2012. So: record the time spent on each card. Build one consistency pair, for example a "most should be freed from" pick against a later card that places the same activity as human-owned. Don't repeat an item word for word; on a short phone survey that reads as a trick. The authors' advice to identify respondents conflicts with the candour we need, so keep responses confidential, report only groups above a minimum size, and rely on these in-survey checks instead.
- **Follow-ups teach people to say "no".** [RCT, validated against records] When follow-up questions came straight after each "yes", respondents learned to say "no" to shorten the survey. Filter questions were triggered 37.9% of the time in that format, against 42.4% when all filters came first, and the filters-first format was more accurate. The effect was significant in one section only. *Source:* Eckman et al. 2014. This is a direct risk for an adaptive card engine. In the current flow, `keep` sends anyone who swipes "It made me" on the pitchbook to an extra card (`how`). That makes the answer the sponsor most needs to hear the one that costs more time. Ask all the screener questions together on one multi-select card, then branch. Keep the follow-ups per "yes" few and predictable. Compare yes-rates on early and late filters as a check.
- Detail is itself a check (Flanagan above). Balanced trade-offs remove the easy staged answer (Cao & Drasgow above).

### Survivorship: we only ask the people who made it

- **Studying survivors makes risky practices look good.** [theory] When only the survivors of a selective process are studied, practices unrelated to success, especially risky or high-variance ones, appear to work, because those they failed have dropped out. *Source:* Denrell 2003. Completers will over-endorse "thrown in front of clients early" and "sink or swim with a demanding Advisor". So: (1) say plainly in the readout that findings describe what completers credit, not what works; (2) ask "Think of someone in your cohort who didn't make it to Advisor. What did they miss, or have too much of?", using the fixed third-person frame; (3) recommend the sponsor run a short parallel pulse with current Analysts and leavers, or compare the results against HR attrition by team.

## Engaging without adding noise

On 25 September Haresh said he did not want "people just clicking on boxes. There have to be sliders, images." Much of the evidence below cuts against that. It is set out plainly, along with where visuals can go without costing data.

**Sliders.** [RCT, several] Drag-to-set sliders lowered response rates, most of all on mobile. They also changed who completed and how answers were distributed, and made responses slower. A tap-to-place line had none of these problems, even on smartphones (Funke 2016). An earlier experiment found sliders raised break-off sharply (odds ratio 6.9; Funke, Reips & Thomas 2011). Where sliders did no harm, they added nothing: a study in a probability panel found measurement quality "quite similar" to radio buttons and "no need to shift" (Bosch et al. 2019). The handle's starting position changed answers, and smartphone users were more sensitive to it (Maineri et al. 2021, N = 6,343 and 4,124). Smartphone users struggled to move small handles to the value they meant (Antoun, Couper & Conrad 2017). A 2026 experiment found tap-style scales had lower error than sliders (Angelike & Reips). Against this, one small study of 74 business managers found no difference in response rate or time (Roster et al. 2015). No study reviewed shows sliders gaining precision.
*When to use one:* never as a drag control, and never for a headline measure. The current `prep` card uses a slider styled as a route, a flag dragged along a mountain track, for the global preparedness rating. That combines the weakest item with the weakest control, and a branch hangs off its value. If the route drawing stays, make it tap-to-place on four or five labelled stops. Draw no flag until the respondent taps. Store an untouched control as missing, never as the midpoint. The labels should carry the meaning, not a 0 to 10 number. For the AI question, use tap buttons with the working group's own categories: Human-owned / Human-led; AI-assisted; AI-led with human review; Reassign, self-serve or eliminate.

**Images.** [RCT] An image on a question screen acts as a comparison standard. Respondents shown a fit woman exercising rated their own health lower than those shown a sick woman in hospital, whatever the image's size and position (Couper, Conrad & Tourangeau 2007). An image of a confident Advisor next to "how ready were you?" will probably pull ratings down. Keep illustrations off any screen that asks for a self-rating. The next two points are our inference, not tested findings. On the `cert` card the pictured options are evaluatively loaded: "a pilot's wings pin" for Yes, "an open door, no gate" for No. The sub-line "Pilots are. Surgeons are." leans the same way. On `keep`, only the pitchbook carries an image, which makes one option more salient than the others. Where an image does functional work, as the thing being sorted, give every option an image of equal weight and neutral tone.

**Complexity and breakoff on phones.** [meta-analysis] Across 39 samples of mobile web surveys, breakoff was lower with mobile optimisation, shorter surveys, less complex design, more reminders, pre-recruitment and a choice of device. Complex design here means grids, drop-downs, sliders, images and progress indicators. Email invitations beat SMS. Stating the duration in the invitation had no effect (Mavletova & Couper 2015). Budget visual elements the way you budget questions. Spend effort on reminders instead. Test on low-end phones over slow connections.

**Gamification.** [RCT; review] A four-way experiment (text, decorative visuals, functional visuals, gamified) found the gamified and functional versions raised satisfaction but made "no real differences in respondent engagement measures" and few differences in response patterns (Downes-Le Guin et al. 2012). A review found game elements reliably make surveys more enjoyable, but their effect on completion, breakoff and satisficing is unclear (Keusch & Zhang 2017). In one study the early advantages of drag interactions faded when respondents took the survey again (Sikkel et al. 2014). Justify the climb metaphor as respect for the respondent, not as a lever on completion. Don't tell the sponsor it will raise response rates. Prefer visuals that do measurement work, such as the token triage board and the human-vs-AI sort, over decorative ones. If the claim matters, measure completion and straightlining against a plain version.

**Length and position.** [RCT] A longer stated length (10, 20 or 30 minutes) meant fewer people started and fewer finished. Answers later in the questionnaire were faster, shorter and more uniform. Block order was randomised, so this is an effect of position (Galesic & Bosnjak 2009). The current flow puts its only open text ("No one should graduate without having...") last and marks it optional. Move it, and the sponsor's core question, into the first third. "Six minutes" must stay true for the longest route through the flow.

**Progress indicators.** [meta-analysis] Across 32 experiments, constant-speed progress bars did not reduce drop-off. Bars that moved fast early and slowly later reduced it, and bars that moved slowly early increased it (Villar, Callegaro & Yang 2013). An adaptive engine cannot know the total number of cards, so a live percentage will stall or jump. Our inference: show no bar, or show section-level progress ("part 2 of 4") with the early sections short. Don't turn the ladder rungs into a percentage.

**Grids, cards and order.** [RCT, several] Splitting a demanding grid into separate items reduced straightlining but took longer (Roßmann, Gummer & Silber 2017). On mobile, several questions per scrolling page were faster than one per screen (Mavletova & Couper 2014). One-per-screen layouts are more sensitive to question order. A priming question shifted answers only when the items were on separate pages (Stefkovics & Kmetty 2022, non-probability panel). Don't grid-rate the deck's thirty or so activities, and don't give each one a card. Use the working group's limited-vote mechanic as one triage screen per capability area; that this beats ratings is our inference. Ask the recall questions ("what built your readiness") before any AI framing appears. Randomise the order of capability areas and log the order each person saw. Keep one presentation format throughout.

**Attention span.** [review; RCT] The "8-second, shorter than a goldfish" claim traces to a Microsoft Canada report citing a website, and a BBC investigation found no underlying research. That history was read in a secondary summary. Evidence for meaningful generational differences at work is "at best, scant" (Rudolph et al. 2020). On phones, respondents multitasked more but were at least as conscientious and as willing to disclose as on a PC. Their problems were with fiddly controls (Antoun, Couper & Conrad 2017). These respondents are high-performing professionals with a stake in the next cohort. They will write one or two considered open answers. What loses them is fiddly controls, slow loading and wasted time.

## Rules for our questions

1. Ask for one specific episode and what the respondent did in it, with a matching recent, ordinary episode, instead of any global rating of how well A2A prepared them (Flanagan; Alaybek; Sitzmann).
2. Ask for dated firsts and the role held (observe / prepare / interpret / present / own), never an amount of change or a count of reps (Schwarz; Macnamara; Anthony; CFP 2019).
3. For every experience a respondent names, capture how much of a stretch it was, whether anyone debriefed it, and whether the feedback was on their reasoning, their output, or them (DeRue & Wellman; Kluger & DeNisi; Wisniewski et al.).
4. Ask whether the respondent formed their own view before seeing a senior's, template's or tool's answer, and what they did with generated drafts: used as-is / checked / rebuilt / asked why (Bastani et al.; Shen & Tamkin; Randazzo et al.).
5. Ask separately which tasks taught the respondent something and which got them into the room or alongside the Advisor (Beane; Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen; Kitces).
6. Frame "which reps stay human" as who owned the framing and the final call, and offer "a tool or example library could have taught me this faster" alongside "I would have lost this" (Randazzo et al.; Dell'Acqua et al.; Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond).
7. Never ask respondents to estimate how AI would have changed their development or speed; record cohort year and treat any AI opinion as an attitude (METR; Bastani et al.).
8. Use balanced best-worst or trade-off cards with item-specific labels in place of importance ratings, drag-to-rank or agree/disagree, pairing options of similar appeal and showing each equally often (Cao & Drasgow; Liang et al.; Saris et al.).
9. Ask sensitive items, including about peers who did not make it, in one fixed third-person frame, and report the answers as perceptions (Fisher; Denrell).
10. Put all screener questions on one multi-select card before any branching, so that no answer shortens the survey (Eckman et al.).
11. After a respondent names their biggest accelerator, ask what else mattered as much and what they undervalued at the time (Roese & Vohs; Bjork & Bjork).
12. Use tap targets only; any continuous visual is tap-to-place on labelled stops, with no starting handle, and an untouched control is stored as missing (Funke; Maineri et al.; Antoun et al.).
13. Keep illustrations off screens that ask for a self-rating or a choice, and give pictured options equal, neutral images (Couper et al. 2007; Mavletova & Couper 2015).
14. Put the sponsor's core question and the main open text in the first third, ask the readiness recall before any AI framing, and randomise and log the order of capability areas (Galesic & Bosnjak; Stefkovics & Kmetty).
15. Show section-level progress or none, never a live percentage, and make the stated length true for the longest route (Villar et al.; Galesic & Bosnjak).
